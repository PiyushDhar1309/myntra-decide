Part 2 · Metric decomposition

Where wishlists stall

Breaking wishlist-to-purchase into the terms that actually move it — and finding that the one nobody owns is the one that decides the outcome.

The metric

M = share of wishlisting users who purchase ≥ 1 saved item
within 30 days of adding it

Three properties of that sentence do most of the work below: it is measured per user, not per item; it asks for at least one, not a rate; and the 30-day clock starts per item, at save.

Those properties decide which interventions can possibly work. A user with forty saved items counts exactly once, whether they buy one thing or six. Which item they buy is irrelevant. So the metric does not reward converting more items — it rewards converting more people, and every person needs only one unblocked purchase to cross the line.

The decomposition

Six terms, multiplied

For any saved item to be bought inside its 30-day window, six things must all hold. They multiply, so the smallest term caps the result — and one of them has no owner at all.

Confidence and Checkout are where the platform has invested. Resolution is the term no team has ever been asked to move — and, as the next section shows, it is structurally the smallest one for exactly the users who save the most.

The arithmetic

Why competing saves cap themselves

Saved items are not independent. When someone saves six comparable kurtas, they are substitutes: at most one will be bought. That places a ceiling on each item's conversion by construction, before any question of fit, price or delivery arises.

For a set C of k substitutable saves:

per item   ci  ≤  1 / k
per user   P(buys ≥1 from C)  =  P(decision resolves) × P(checkout | resolved)

k disappears from the second line. Which item wins does not matter to the metric — only that one wins.

At the item level, resolving a six-way shortlist looks like destroying five sales. At the user level, it is the difference between one and zero.

This is, I think, why the feature has never been built. Item-level thinking makes comparison look value-destroying, and a merchandising team measured on items would be right to refuse it. The metric we have been handed is measured per user, which inverts the conclusion.

It also produces a testable prediction, and an uncomfortable one: conversion per saved item should fall as a wishlist grows, because each addition both divides attention and raises k. Saving more should help a user — more chances at "at least one" — but only if the extra saves are independent. When they are substitutes, they compete with each other rather than adding to each other.

Underneath the metric

The behaviours doing the damage

Every term above is a product outcome. Each is driven by something a person actually does, observed in the discovery corpus:

Saving several near-identical items in order to choose between them later Depresses Resolution. The defining behaviour: one decision stored as many intents.
Grouping saved items into collections by hand The existing workaround. Proves the need, and stops at filing.
Deferring until a trigger — a sale, a restock, an occasion Depresses Availability and Recall. Deliberate waiting, not lost interest.
Leaving the platform to resolve doubt Depresses Confidence, and hides the resolution from Myntra entirely.
Never revisiting older saves once the list is long Depresses Recall. The list grows monotonically; attention does not.
Using the wishlist as an inspiration board Caps Intent. These saves are in the denominator and were never going to convert.
Where the opportunity is

Resolution, and only resolution

Checkout is optimised. Confidence has a live feature. Availability has a functioning third-party market, which is its own evidence of demand. Intent and Recall are real gaps but diffuse — hard to attribute, hard to move in one release.

Resolution is unowned, structurally small, and concentrated. It bites hardest on the heaviest wishlist users, who are the platform's most engaged shoppers, and it is the one term where a single successful intervention flips a user from zero to one — which is exactly what the metric counts.

Before building anything

Five queries that would size this

None of the above needs a model to verify — it needs five days with the wishlist tables.

  1. How much of the wishlist is competing with itself? Share of saved items sitting in a comparable set — same subcategory, prices within roughly 2×, three or more items — and the share of users with at least one such set. If that number is small, this whole thesis is wrong and should be abandoned.
  2. Do clustered saves convert worse than lone ones? 30-day per-item conversion for clustered versus singleton saves, stratified by wishlist size so the two effects are separated. The prediction: clustered converts worse, and the gap widens with cluster size.
  3. Do these decisions ever resolve on their own? For users holding a cluster, the share who eventually buy exactly one from it, and the time-to-purchase distribution. This is the counterfactual any experiment is measured against.
  4. Does filing already help? Myntra is running this experiment already. Compare conversion of items inside a user-made collection against that same user's unfiled saves. If organising alone lifts conversion, deciding should lift it further.
  5. What share of saves were ever purchase intent? Proxied by revisit rate and add-to-bag rate within 30 days. This sets the realistic ceiling on M, and stops the target being set against saves that were never going to convert.
