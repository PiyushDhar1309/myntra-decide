Part 7 · Risks and mitigation

Why this might fail

Seven ways a wishlist comparison feature goes wrong — two of them created by our own design, one of them organisational rather than technical — and what we would do about each.

Ordered by how likely each is to end the project, not by how uncomfortable they are to write down.

The register
01Clustering is wrong, and a wrong group is worse than nonewould kill it
The failure
We suggest grouping six things the person was never choosing between — a work kurta and a wedding kurta, say. It doesn't just fail to help; it says this system does not understand me, and people stop reading the surface entirely.
Why likely
Subcategory and price are crude proxies for "substitutable". Occasion, formality and intent are what actually make two items rivals, and none of them is reliably in the catalog.
Early signal
Suggestion acceptance rate, visible within days. It is the first number to read and the cheapest to act on.
Mitigation
Tune for precision over recall — miss half the real sets rather than surface one absurd one. Let people add and remove items from a set, which both fixes the immediate error and generates labelled training data. And sequence it: ship against collections a human already made before auto-detecting anything.
02Too few wishlists actually contain a decisionwould kill it
The failure
If most saved items are unrelated one-offs, the addressable population is small and the ceiling on the business metric is low however good the feature is.
Why likely
Heavy wishlisters may be hoarding inspiration rather than shortlisting. Both behaviours look identical in the data until you define a set and count.
Early signal
Knowable before writing any code — the coverage query in Part 6 runs against existing wishlist tables.
Mitigation
Run that query first and let it decide. This is not really a mitigation so much as a gate: if coverage is small, do not build this, and say so.
03The decision was never the blockerwould kill it
The failure
People happily use the comparison, pick a winner, and still don't buy — because what was really stopping them was price, or trust in returns, or doubt about the fabric. We resolved the decision and moved nothing.
Why likely
Quality and fabric doubt ranks highest of all decision factors in the discovery corpus. It is entirely possible that choice is a visible symptom of an information problem.
Early signal
High compare completion with flat conversion — the pattern named as the stop condition in Part 6.
Mitigation
None, and that is the point. This one is not engineered around: it sends you back to the decomposition to pick a different term. Building the instrumentation so you can tell is the mitigation.
04We steer people cheapwould shrink it
The failure
The comparison ticks the lowest price. Repeated across millions of decisions that is a systematic nudge toward the cheapest option — conversion rises, revenue per user falls, and we have optimised our own metric at the company's expense.
Why likely
We built it. A green tick next to the lowest number is read as an endorsement whatever the caption says.
Early signal
Order value on wishlist-sourced purchases, against control.
Mitigation
Test a variant with no tick on price — it is stated plainly in the row already. Better still, let the shopper choose which attribute leads the comparison, so the weighting is theirs rather than ours. Hard stop threshold on order value, agreed before launch.
05We talk someone out of their second purchasewould shrink it
The failure
Collapsing six saved items to one is only a win if they were never going to buy two. Where they would have, we have converted a person and lost a unit.
Why likely
The whole mechanic is elimination. Pressing "pick one" is a fairly direct instruction not to buy the others.
Early signal
Items per order, against control. This is the single guardrail most likely to trip.
Mitigation
Never frame it as discarding. The copy is "pick one to buy now", and parked items stay one tap away rather than being deleted. Allow keeping two as a legitimate resolution rather than forcing a single winner.
06Brands object to being visibly beatenwould slow it
The failure
A side-by-side comparison that ticks a winner puts one brand partner visibly ahead of another inside Myntra's own interface. Marketplace and brand-relations teams have legitimate standing to block that, and this is the sort of objection that surfaces late and stops launches.
Why likely
Brands pay for placement and prominence. A feature that ranks them on delivery speed and rating cuts directly across that commercial relationship.
Early signal
Not a metric. Take it to those teams in design review, not after a successful test.
Mitigation
The defensible line is already in the design: we only ever compare items the shopper saved themselves. We never inject a competitor, never recommend, and never tick anything subjective — no ticks on fabric, colour or pattern. This is the user's own shortlist organised, not Myntra picking sides. That distinction is worth holding even where it costs us a useful feature.
07The catalog can't support a comparisonwould slow it
The failure
Comparison needs structured attributes that are present, consistent and comparable. Real catalogs have gaps, free-text variants and disagreement between sellers. A table full of blanks is worse than no table.
Why likely
Marketplace listings are supplied by thousands of sellers. Our own prototype had to confront this — the honest answer there was to show a dash rather than estimate.
Early signal
Attribute completeness by category, measurable today.
Mitigation
Only render rows where every item in the set has a value — degrade to fewer, better rows rather than showing holes. Launch in the categories with the most complete data. And the aggregated gaps become real leverage on sellers to fill them in, which is a second-order win.
The master mitigation

Sequence it so the riskiest part ships last

Most of the register above is really one risk wearing different clothes: we do not yet know what a real decision set looks like. The sequencing answers several at once by learning that before betting on it.

Phase 0 · a week Query, don't buildRun the coverage and baseline queries from Part 6 against historical wishlist data. This alone resolves risk 02, produces the counterfactual, and sets an honest ceiling. If coverage is thin, the project stops here having cost a week.
Phase 1 Compare inside existing collections onlyEvery set is one a human already declared. Clustering risk is zero, the population is small but real, and we learn what people actually compare on before automating anything.
Phase 2 Suggest groupings, measured on acceptanceNow auto-detection ships, tuned on what Phase 1 taught us and gated on acceptance rate rather than on conversion. Wrong groups get caught cheaply and early.
Phase 3 Full experiment on the business metricOnly once the mechanism is known to work do we spend eight weeks measuring whether it pays.
Honesty

The risk in this document itself

The problem definition rests on research not yet done

Parts 2 and 4 argue that Resolution is the binding term, on the strength of a discovery corpus and an argument. The primary research that would confirm or refute it is still outstanding, and a kill criterion was set in advance precisely because writing the conclusion first is the failure mode of every case study.

This project has already abandoned one fully built MVP after discovering Myntra Fit Assist shipped. That was expensive, and it is the reason risks 02 and 03 are written as gates rather than as things to manage around.

  • If the interviews contradict the thesis, Part 4 gets rewritten around quality and fabric doubt — the largest decision factor in the corpus, and the one with no live feature against it.
  • If coverage is thin, the honest recommendation is not to build this, and the case study should say so rather than shipping a feature to justify the work.
