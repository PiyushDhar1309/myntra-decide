How we'd know it worked

One business metric, one product metric we're accountable for, five things that move first, and seven that must not get worse — each defined precisely enough to compute.

Business metric

Stated so it can be computed

Wishlist conversionnorth star
Numerator
Users who purchased at least one item that was in their wishlist, where the purchase fell within 30 days of that item's save timestamp
Denominator
Users who added at least one item to their wishlist in the period
Window
Rolling, per item — the clock starts at each save, not at period start
Grain
Per user, not per item. Forty saves and one purchase counts the same as forty saves and six
Why
Given by the brief. Its grain is the single most important fact in this document: it means we do not need to convert more items, only more people — and every person needs exactly one unblocked purchase
Product metric

The one number this team is accountable for

Decision resolution rate (DRR)primary
Set
Three or more items saved by one user in the same subcategory whose prices sit within roughly 2× of each other. Formed at the moment the third item joins
Resolved
Within 30 days of formation, either (a) one item from the set is purchased, or (b) the set is deliberately reduced to two or fewer live items
Formula
DRR = sets resolved within 30d ÷ sets formed
DCR = sets resolved by purchase ÷ sets formed
Why two
DRR tells you the mechanism works — people can now finish a decision. DCR tells you it pays. Reporting only DCR would reward us for pushing purchases; reporting only DRR would let us celebrate helping people buy nothing
Not resolved
Keeping every item in the set. The wishlist is the same size it was, so nothing was decided — and counting it would let this team satisfy DRR without any behaviour changing. The prototype says so on screen rather than quietly recording a win
Baseline
Computable retrospectively, from wishlist tables, before anything ships. The set definition is just a query — so the counterfactual is knowable without an experiment
Why this metric and not "compare views"

Engagement with a feature is not the outcome; a comparison screen someone opens and abandons has made things worse, not better. DRR is defined on the user's unit of work — the decision — and it can get worse when we ship badly. A metric that cannot go down is not a metric.

Which is also why keeping everything is excluded. The easiest way to make this number look good is to count any completed comparison, and that is exactly the version of it that would let us report success while the wishlist stayed the same size.

Leading indicators

What moves before the thirty days are up

DRR takes a month per cohort. These move in days, and each one isolates a different way the feature could be failing — which matters, because "it didn't work" is not an actionable result.

IndicatorDefinitionWhat it isolates
Suggestion acceptanceGrouping suggestions accepted ÷ shown Whether the clustering is any good. If this is low nothing downstream matters — we are grouping things people were not choosing between
Compare entrySets where compare is opened ÷ sets with 3+ items Discovery and intent. Low means they never found it, or did not want it
Compare completionCompare sessions ending in a pick ÷ sessions started Whether the comparison actually helps. Abandonment here means we surfaced the wrong differences
Time to decisionMedian days from set formed to resolved Speed, independent of rate. A feature that resolves the same sets faster still moves the 30-day metric
Park-not-deleteItems parked ÷ items removed from sets Whether elimination-without-loss works. If people still delete, the anxiety was not the barrier we thought
Escalation to pairsSessions using two-at-a-time ÷ compare sessions Whether the table alone is sufficient. High escalation means the table is not doing its job and should be redesigned
Guardrails

The ways this could win and still lose

Each of these is a specific, plausible failure — not a generic health check.

GuardrailDefinitionThe failure it catches
Items per orderMean units per wishlist-sourced order The main one. We may talk someone out of a second purchase they would have made. Collapsing six to one is only a win if they were never buying two
Order valueMean value of wishlist-sourced orders The comparison ticks the lowest price. That could systematically steer people cheap and raise conversion while lowering revenue — a real risk created by our own design
Wishlist addsItems saved per active user per week If a "decide" prompt makes saving feel like homework, people save less — and saving is upstream of every part of this

Suggestion dismissalSuggestions dismissed ÷ shown Noise. A prompt people reflexively dismiss trains them to ignore the surface

Revenue per wishlisting userWishlist-sourced revenue ÷ wishlisting users The backstop. Converts the whole argument into money and catches anything the metrics above miss
Target setting

What we can honestly promise

Not a number picked to sound good. The achievable lift is bounded by two things we can measure before building — how many users actually have an unresolved decision, and how many of those would not have converted anyway.

Δ(business metric)  ≤  C × I

C = share of wishlisting users holding ≥1 unresolved set and no other wishlist purchase in the window
I = incremental resolution rate — sets we resolve that would not have resolved unaided

C comes from the retrospective query. I is the only thing the experiment has to discover, because the unaided resolution rate is already in the historical data. That makes the target a measurement rather than a guess — and it caps the promise honestly: if only 12% of users hold an unresolved set, no amount of good design moves the metric more than 12 points.

The experiment

How we'd actually run it

  • Unit: the user, not the session or the set — the business metric is per-user, so anything else invites a mismatch between what we test and what we report.
  • Eligibility: only users with at least one detected set. Diluting the sample with people who have nothing to decide buries a real effect in noise.
  • Primary: business metric, 30-day window. Secondary: DRR and DCR. Read first: the six leading indicators.
  • Duration: at least eight weeks — a 30-day outcome window plus a burn-in for novelty to decay, or we will ship a result that was really curiosity.
  • Stop early if: items per order or order value degrade beyond a pre-agreed threshold. Set that threshold before launch, not while looking at the dashboard.
The result that would make us stop

Business metric flat while compare engagement is healthy. That would mean people are using the feature and it is not changing what they buy — the decision was never the binding constraint, and the honest response is to go back to Part 2 and pick a different term rather than iterate on the UI.
