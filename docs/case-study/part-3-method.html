Part 3 · Primary research

The wishlist walkthrough

A protocol for eight interviews with heavy wishlisters — run against their own wishlist, on their own phone, item by item.

Method

Walk the list, don't discuss it

The whole design rests on one choice: every question is asked about a specific item they can see on their screen, never about shopping in general. Asked abstractly, "do you compare things?" produces an opinion. Asked against the fourth kurta in their list, "why is this one here?" produces evidence.

Eight people walking roughly eight items each gives about 60 item-level observations, not eight anecdotes — enough to say "19 of 61 saved items sat in a set the person was actively choosing between" rather than "users told us they compare."

The one way to ruin this round: asking about alternatives too early. If you open with "are you comparing these?", everyone agrees and you have learned nothing. Reason for saving is asked open, first. Alternatives come at question 6, after they have already told you in their own words.

Segment

Heavy wishlisters

Include30+ items currently saved on Myntra IncludeAdded something in the last two weeks — the list is live, not abandoned IncludeNo purchase from the wishlist in the last month ExcludeFewer than 30 saved — a different behaviour ExcludeBought from the wishlist this week — their decision loop already works Aim forA spread of ages and both women's and men's shopping, so the finding isn't one demographic

This segment was chosen because it tests the hardest half of the thesis — that a list which only grows converts worse per item — and because it is the group most likely to contradict us by revealing that many saves were never purchase intent at all. That answer is worth having before Part 6 sets a target.

Screener

Four questions, asked before you book

  1. Do you shop on Myntra? How often, roughly? Looking for at least monthly. Anyone who has drifted off the platform will be reconstructing from memory.
  2. Open your wishlist — roughly how many things are in there? Make them look. Estimates are wildly wrong in both directions. Need 30+.
  3. When did you last buy something that had been sitting in there? Want "a while ago" or "can't remember". Bought this week means their loop is working.
  4. Have you added anything in the last two weeks? Confirms the list is live. A dormant list produces archaeology, not decisions.
The guide · about 40 minutes

Five blocks

Setting up2 min

Say: I'm not selling anything and I didn't build this, so being blunt helps me. There are no right answers. Can I record? Then — please open your Myntra wishlist and keep it in front of you; we're going to go through it together.

1 · The list as a whole5 min
Tell me about your wishlist. What's in there? Open. Let them characterise it before you impose any frame.
What do you actually use it for? Tests Intent. Listen for "to keep track", "to decide later", "so I don't lose it".
How long have things been sitting in there?
When did you last buy something out of it? What made that one happen? The one resolution they can remember. Often the most useful minute of the interview.
Do you ever clear it out? What happens to things that don't get bought? Tests whether any elimination mechanism exists at all.
2 · Item walkthrough — the core20 min

Pick 8 items: the 3 most recent, the 3 oldest, and 2 they point at. Ask all seven questions per item, in this order. Capture verbatim.

Why did you save this one? Open, and asked first for a reason. This single answer codes the item.
Do you still want it?
What's stopping you buying it right now?
What would have to happen for you to buy it?
Is there anything you still need to know about it? Separates an information gap from a choice gap. Critical distinction for Part 4.
Is there anything else in your list that's a bit like this one? Only now. Let them scroll and find it themselves — don't point.
If yes — how would you pick between them? Have you tried? The heart of it. Listen for whether they have ever attempted the comparison, and what stopped them.
3 · Outside the app6 min
Between saving something and buying it, do you ever go and look somewhere else?
Where do you go, and what are you trying to find out?
Has that ever changed your mind about something in here? Resolution happening off-platform, which Myntra never sees.
4 · What they already do about it5 min
Have you ever made a collection, or a list, or grouped things? Myntra ships collections. Adoption and reasons for non-adoption both matter.
Did it help? What did you do with it after making it? Testing the "filing is not deciding" claim directly. Do not lead them to it.
Any other way you keep track — screenshots, notes, sending things to someone?
Have you ever bought two things meaning to return one?
5 · Closing3 min
If your wishlist could do one thing it doesn't do today, what would you pick?
Is there anything I should have asked and didn't?

Analysis

Six numbers, and the one that kills it

  1. Share of walked items whose stated reason was comparing The headline. Coded from their own words, before alternatives were mentioned.
  2. Share of items sitting in a self-identified alternative set They found the alternatives themselves. Stronger than any clustering algorithm.
  3. Share of respondents with at least one such set Maps to the user-level metric — coverage of the intervention.
  4. Choice versus information as the blocker If information dominates, the opportunity is Confidence, not Resolution, and Part 4 changes.
  5. How many have ever resolved a set on their own The counterfactual. If they resolve unaided, the feature buys speed, not conversion.
  6. Share of saves that were never purchase intent The realistic ceiling on the business metric, and an honest input to Part 6.

Kill criterion, set in advance. If fewer than roughly a third of live saved items sit in a set the person is choosing between, and choice is not among the leading blockers, then Resolution is not the gap — and Part 4 should follow the evidence to quality and fabric doubt, or to intent that was never really there. Deciding this before the interviews is what stops the analysis becoming a search for agreement.
