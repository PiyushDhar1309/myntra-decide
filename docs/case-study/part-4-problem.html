Part 4 · Problem definition

The shortlist that never ends

Myntra's wishlist stores items. Its users are holding decisions. Nothing in the product can close one, so they stay open — and open decisions do not convert.

How the thinking moved

Six beats, one of them a dead end

Business metric Buy ≥1 saved item within 30 days — measured per user Read closely, the wording decides everything downstream. It counts people, not items, and asks for at least one. A user with forty saves counts exactly once, and which item they buy is irrelevant.
Product outcomes Six multiplicative terms, and one with no owner Intent × Recall × Availability × Confidence × Resolution × Checkout. Checkout is optimised, Confidence has a live feature, Availability has a third-party industry filling the gap. Resolution is unowned.
AI discovery The gap is deliberate waiting, not lost interest Across 5,000+ rows of public feedback, people describe saving in order to compare before choosing — one complains the wishlist size cap gets in the way of exactly that. Intent is intact and parked on a condition, which makes every "complete your purchase" nudge an answer to a question nobody asked.
Abandoned Fit confidence for non-standard bodies Six interviews pointed here, and a full MVP was built and deployed against it. Then Myntra Fit Assist turned out to already recommend sizes from order history. The work was thrown away rather than defended. Checking what already ships is now the first question, not the last.
Primary research Seven heavy wishlisters — 49 to 89 saved items each 7 of 7 save several of the same thing rather than one. 5 of 7 said "compare" unprompted when asked what they do after saving — a question that never mentions comparing. 6 of 7 have never made a collection. But the most common stated blocker was price, not choice, and 0 of 7 were bothered by the size of their list.
Problem The wishlist has no way to end a decision Its only two exits assume the choice is already made.

The abandoned branch stays on the page deliberately. A reviewer who opens the Myntra app will find Fit Assist in a minute; better they see it was found and acted on.

Product outcome

Decision resolution rate

The share of competing saved sets that reach a decision — bought, or deliberately released — within 30 days of the set forming.

It is not measured today, because the product has no concept of a set. That is the first thing to fix: you cannot move a number nobody computes.

Root cause

Only two exits, and both assume you have already chosen

Shortlisting is an elimination process. You add candidates in order to remove them until one is left. Myntra's wishlist offers exactly two ways out — buy and delete — and both require the decision to have been made somewhere else, in the user's head, unaided.

Delete is the only elimination the product provides, and it reads as discarding something you did work to find. So nobody uses it for that. The list only grows.

The mismatch underneath

The product's unit is the item. The user's unit is the decision. Six saved kurtas are one deferred choice stored as six independent intents — so the system sees six things it can nudge, when there is really one thing that has to resolve. Until it does, none of the six converts, and every nudge is aimed at the wrong object.

This is a cause, not a symptom, because fixing anything else leaves it intact. Perfect size guidance on all six kurtas does not tell you which one to buy. Neither does a price drop on three of them, or faster delivery on all of them.

Existing workarounds

What people already do about it

Every one of these is a cost users absorb because the product will not.

Grouping saved items into collections by hand Shipped by Myntra and adopted — which proves the need. But filing is not deciding: a "Wedding" folder with six kurtas in it is no closer to one purchase.
Installing third-party wishlist trackers Flipshope, GlassIt, PriceHistory and others exist to watch Myntra wishlists. People install external software to make the feature work.
Leaving the platform to resolve doubt Instagram, forums, competitor sites. The resolution happens where Myntra cannot see it, and sometimes ends in someone else's checkout.
Buying two and returning one — 5 of 7 Four did it for size. One did it purely to compare: "when I like two items very much I buy them and compare which looks better on me." Paying in money and reverse logistics for a comparison the product would not host.
Asking someone else to decide "Sometimes I also ask my friends about the product I should purchase." The decision leaves the platform entirely.
Why it matters to the user

A list that can finish

The value is not "shop more", and it is not tidiness either — the research was clear that a long list bothers nobody. 0 of 7 minded having 49 to 89 things saved, and several said they liked having the options. What they cannot do is finish. Every workaround above costs real time, and the most common one costs money and a courier.

The point is not to help someone buy six things, nor to tidy the list. It is to let one unmade decision actually end.
Why it makes business sense

The case, including the objection

  • The metric rewards it directly. Per user, at least one purchase — so breaking a single logjam converts the whole person, regardless of which item wins.
  • It costs no margin. Pure decision support. Nothing here touches price, which is the constraint the brief imposed.
  • It completes an asset Myntra already owns. Collections did the hard half — getting people to group things. The missing half is cheap by comparison.
  • It should reduce returns. Every buy-two-return-one is a comparison the product refused to host, paid for twice in reverse logistics.
  • It concentrates on the most engaged users, where retention value is highest and acquisition cost is already sunk.
The objection this has to survive

Helping someone collapse six saved items into one purchase looks like destroying five sales, and a team measured on items would be right to refuse it. But six unresolved items convert at approximately zero, and the metric asks only whether the person bought something. At item level this reads as value-destroying. At user level it is the difference between one and nil — and that is almost certainly why it has never been built.

Falsification

What would make me drop this

  • If under roughly a third of live saved items sit in a set the person is choosing between, the problem is not big enough to be the answer.
  • If information rather than choice is the dominant blocker, the opportunity is Confidence — quality and fabric doubt, which the corpus ranks highest of all decision factors — and this document should be rewritten around that.
  • If people resolve their sets unaided within the window, the feature buys speed rather than conversion, and the business case shrinks to something much smaller.
  • If most saves turn out never to have been purchase intent, the ceiling on the metric is low and Part 6 must be honest about it rather than setting a target that cannot be hit.

Resolved against the interviews, which were run after these were written. The first did not trip: every participant saves competing sets deliberately. The second half-tripped — information is not the dominant blocker, but price is, ahead of choice, so a comparison feature alone cannot convert this segment and Part 6 caps the promise accordingly. The fourth is the one to watch: most saves here were real intent, but the wishlist is also being used as a waiting room for sales, which no non-monetary feature resolves.
